# Inca Mythology, Religion & Legend — Research Dossier
**For:** Narrative designer building a Tomb Raider-style action-adventure game set in the modern Peruvian Andes
**Protagonist:** Naira Quispe, Peruvian archaeologist hunting Paititi
**Antagonists:** Sol Negro, a black-market antiquities ring
**Compiled:** 2026-09-29 | Read-type flags: `verified live` = page text read directly in this session; `index` = search-index content

**Accuracy policy:** Every fact is cited with its source and read-type. Where scholars disagree or sources are thin, this is flagged in **bold labels**: **[scholarly consensus]**, **[chronicler account]** (16th–17th c. Spanish sources, often filtered through conquest politics), **[modern interpretation]** (present-day Andean/revivalist readings not documented pre-conquest), **[fringe]** (speculative, no scholarly support). Two facts with directly conflicting sources are reported as conflicts, not resolved silently.

**One critical framing note:** The Inca Empire lasted roughly a century (c. 1438–1532/1572). Its "mythology" is largely an imperial overlay on far older Andean beliefs (Chavín, Moche, Nazca, Wari, Huarochirí). Almost all written sources are Spanish chroniclers' accounts or post-conquest Quechua texts; pre-conquest records were burned. Designers should treat the dossier as "what the Inca state claimed + what older Andean cultures believed," not one clean canon.

## 1. The Full Pantheon

The Inca permitted conquered peoples to keep their gods, so the pantheon is a crowded imperial collage. The state cult in Cusco centered on **five paramount entities** that received the vast majority of state sacrifices, maintained by the ten royal *panacas* (lineages) in a rotating ritual calendar: **the Sun, the Moon, Thunder, Viracocha, and the Earth** (`index`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_the_Inca_Empire).

### The great gods

**Viracocha** (Apu Qun Tiqsi Wiraqutra — "lord, foundation, fat of the sea")
- Creator of all things, or "the substance from which all things are created"; intimately associated with the sea; rose from Lake Titicaca. In pre-Inca iconography he is the **Staff God** (front-facing figure holding staffs), chief deity of the Andes since the Early Horizon (900–200 BC). Supreme creator and "architect of Andean civilization"; sacrifices to him rationalized cosmic/existential events. Notably, **commoners were forbidden to speak his name** — which chroniclers suggest is why the Incas needed multiple foundation legends instead of one (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_mythology).
- **Story hook:** The god whose name cannot be spoken is a perfect cipher for the game's deepest secret. Sol Negro could be hunting "the nameless god's" shrine — and Naira discovers the name-taboo is itself a lock: a quipu or carved inscription that must be *read aloud correctly* (or deliberately *not* read) to open a door. Viracocha walking west across the Pacific also mirrors the "departed god who will return" motif — a rival prophecy to Inkarri.

**Inti** (Apu Inti / Apu Punchaw)
- The Sun; "considered the most important god" of the Inca state; source of warmth, light, protection. The **Sapa Inca claimed direct lineal descent from Inti**, making the sun the "overarching institutional organization of the state" — divinity as government. Gold was his sacred metal: **gold = the sweat of the sun** (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_mythology; `index`, Google Arts & Culture exhibit https://artsandculture.google.com/story/inca-religion-and-archaeoastronomy-universidad-nacional-de-san-antonio-abad-del-cusco-unsaac/jwWRECr11WMRIQ?hl=en).
- Worship: Temple of the Sun at Coricancha (golden disk *Punchao*, carried out at dawn); the Inti Raymi festival; solar alignments everywhere.
- **Archetypal overlap:** Divine-king solar descent rhymes with Egypt (Pharaoh as son of Ra) and Japan (Amaterasu's line). The Book of Symbols' solar archetype — vitality, consciousness, enlightenment — maps cleanly onto Inti as the ordering, civilizing force. Naira, as a modern Peruvian, can inherit this ambivalently: the sun as both national symbol (it's on Peru's flag) and imperial propaganda.
- **Story hook:** Inti's descent-line gives Sol Negro a pseudo-legitimate claim angle: a villain who argues the antiquities "belong to the bloodline" — or a forged genealogy. The Punchao disk (carried out each dawn, returned at night) is a natural "artifact that must be returned to its place" quest object.

**Mama Quilla (Mama Killa)** — the Moon
- Marriage, festival, and moon goddess; daughter of Wiracocha and Mama Qucha; **wife and sister of Inti**. Regulated the calendar, festivals, and women's cycles; patron of women. Silver was her metal (the moon's counterpart to the sun's gold). In one legend she is mother of Manco Qhapaq, Pacha Kamaq, Kon, and Mama Ocllo. Illapa's myth has him drawing water from the Milky Way in a jar (*urpu*) and entrusting it to his sister Mama Quilla (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_mythology).
- Worship: Temple of the Moon at Coricancha with silver murals; lunar calendar alongside the solar one.
- **Story hook:** Moon/silver vs. sun/gold duality is a built-in puzzle language (day/night doors, eclipse mechanics, silver-keyed locks). Mama Quilla as "keeper of the jar of waters" makes her the patron of any water-level puzzle — and her calendar role ties her to the lunar-calendar half of Inca timekeeping (see §9).

**Pachamama (Mama Pacha)** — the Earth
- Sacred mother of hills and men; protector of nature, provider of water and food; the empire's harvests depended on her cult — she received the greatest popular veneration. Kind but dangerous: she shows anger through **droughts, earthquakes, and ruined weather** when disrespected. Depicted as an adult woman carrying potatoes and coca leaves; also as a dragon; symbolized by the spiral. In some versions she is wife of Pachakamaq, and their union (earth + sky) produced Inti and Mama Killa, "the Willkas" (sacred ones) (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_mythology).
- Worship: offerings of food, chicha, coca before planting/harvest; earth itself as recipient. Still venerated in the Andes today.
- **Archetypal overlap:** The Earth Mother is near-universal (Gaia, Tonantzin, etc.). Pachamama's *conditional* benevolence — nourishing mother who punishes neglect — is the classic ambivalent Great Mother. Her spiral symbol echoes the archetypal spiral of growth/cycles.
- **Story hook:** Pachamama is the moral spine of the story. Naira offers to the earth (as real Andean travelers still do); Sol Negro's looting *literally* desecrates her. Earthquakes/droughts as her wrath give the game a diegetic disaster system. The spiral can be a recurring glyph marking safe ground vs. "angry earth" zones.

**Illapa** (Apu Illapa / Chuquiylla / Intillapa)
- Thunder, lightning, rain, and **war**; lord of weather; **third most important god**, after Wiracocha and Inti. An imposing man in brilliant gold and precious stones dwelling in the upper world; carries a *warak'a* (sling) to make storms and a golden *makana* (lightning/thunder/lightning trinity); also depicted as a star-formed warrior. He draws water from the Milky Way into an *urpu* jar and hurls a projectile to shatter it — thunder is the roar, sparks the lightning, spilled water the rain. **To summon rain, priests tied up black dogs and starved them until their sobs moved Illapa to pity** — but if a dog died, his wrath fell as a killing lightning bolt. Rites were held on the highest mountains: dances, chants, festivals, animal sacrifice (human offerings in dire need). He manifested on earth as a **puma or hawk**. His temple *Pucamarca* stood in Cusco; the church of San Blas was built over his shrine; Sacsayhuamán served as both fortress and ceremonial temple with Illapa prominent (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_mythology).
- **Story hook:** The black-dog rite is one of the most gameable rituals in the dossier: a storm-summoning mechanic with a moral cost (the dog must suffer but not die). Illapa's sling-and-jar cosmology is a literal puzzle blueprint — a mechanism where the player "breaks the jar in the sky" to release water. His puma/hawk manifestations let a boss or guardian shift forms. High-mountain shrines = natural level design.

**Pachakamaq (Pachacamac)** — the Earth-Shaker
- Chthonic creator god, "soul/maker/shaker of the earth"; fire, sky, clouds, **earthquakes**. The ancient Peruvians believed **a single movement of his head caused cataclysms; if he moved fully, the world would end**. He *caused* earthquakes rather than preventing them — he had to be appeased so he would *not* send the scourge. Represented by a long **two-faced wooden idol** (duality); priests entered his sanctuary walking backwards; no one could look him in the eye. A coastal (Ichma) god adopted into the Inca pantheon; defeated the god Kon in a primordial battle; creator of the second generation of humans. His great oracle sanctuary at **Pachacamac** near Lima drew pilgrims empire-wide (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_mythology).
- **Story hook:** A god you must approach *backwards*, whose full movement ends the world, is a stealth/traversal set piece waiting to happen (walk the sanctuary in reverse; freeze when the idol "moves"). The two-faced idol is a duality boss. The oracle at Pachacamac can deliver the game's central prophecy — with the classic oracle's double meaning.

**Mama Sara (Saramama)** — maize mother
- Goddess of grain; associated with **maize that grew in multiples or in strange forms — such plants were dressed as little dolls of Mama Sara**; also associated with willow trees. Two constellations served her: *Kuka Manka* (coca-cup, guardian of magical herbs) and *Sara Manka* (corn-cup, guardian of plant foods) (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_mythology).
- **Story hook:** The "strange maize dressed as dolls" is a haunting collectible/ward item. Dark-cloud constellations as guardians = a star-map puzzle in the Milky Way (§7).

**Kon (Wakon)** — the boneless god
- God of rain and wind from the south; **son of Inti and Mama Killa**; "the boneless god" — light, without bones or meat, yet human-formed; also the "flying feline" (feline face/masks) and the "eyed god" (prominent eyes); carried trophy heads and a staff; depicted as a man with bird traits or a feline with prominent eyes. He could **transform into a sandstorm** to cross the coastal deserts. He created the *first* generation of humans — until his **defeat and exile by Pachakamaq** (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_mythology).
- **Story hook:** A defeated creator god exiled to the desert is a superb "wronged deity" antagonist-or-ally. A boneless, shapeshifting sandstorm god gives you a desert chase sequence and a boss that cannot be struck (no bones to break). His trophy heads + staff are distinctive artifact designs.

**Mama Qucha (Mama Cocha)** — the sea
- Sea and fish goddess, protector of sailors and fishermen; in one legend she and Wiracocha parented Inti and Mama Killa; one of the **four elemental mothers** (with Mama Nina/fire, Pachamama/earth, Mama Wayra/winds); she, Pachamama, and Mama Killa together form the **three phases of the Moon** (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_mythology).
- **Story hook:** The three-mothers-as-moon-phases is a combination-lock concept. As a coastal deity, she anchors any ocean/lake levels (Titicaca) and gives Sol Negro a maritime smuggling vector.

### Lesser deities & regional powers (the deep bench)
- **Supay** — god of death (also pests, floods, droughts); "respected rather than rejected" — death as a power to honor, not a devil to flee (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_mythology; `index` video summary). *Later syncretized with the Christian devil* — a colonial distortion worth noting.
- **Urcuchillay** — protector of llama herds, worshipped by herders; golden llama images (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_mythology).
- **Catequil (Apocatequil)** — northern highlands god of day, good, thunder and lightning; born from hatched eggs with twin **Piguerao** (night/evil); possibly another face of Illapa — a genuine theological rhyme across regions (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_mythology).
- **Paryaqaqa** — water/storm god of the Yauyos, adopted via the Huanca into the Inca pantheon; **born as a falcon**, became human (Kolash, "human from the nest"); defeated the child-devouring fire god **Huallallo Carhuincho** (a human with dog traits, god of the Wankas, exiled by Viracocha, later punished — tied hand and foot on an island, bitten eternally). When Huallallo killed Paryaqaqa's son Amaru (lover of Huaytapallana), Paryaqaqa sent a drowning flood; **Viracocha punished both by turning them into snowy mountains** (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_mythology).
- **Cavillace & Coniraya** — the virgin goddess Cavillace ate a fruit that was the moon god Coniraya's seed; her baby crawled to its true father; shamed, she fled to the coast and turned herself and her child into rocks (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_mythology).
- **Ch'aska (Chasca Quyllur)** — Venus/dawn goddess of beauty, virgin women, flowers. **Chuychu (K'uychi)** — the rainbow, "god of the nobles." **Qhoa** — a giant winged flying feline of the sky; lightning shoots from its eyes, its roar is thunder, its urine is rain, its wingbeats are hail (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_mythology).
- **Ekeko** — hearth and wealth god; dolls bearing miniature versions of one's desires grant what you place on them. **Mama Kuka (Mama Coca)** — goddess of coca, health, happiness; born when a promiscuous woman was torn in half by jealous lovers and the coca plant sprang from her body. **Axomama** (potatoes), **Mama Quinoa** (quinoa), **Mama Rayhuana** (flora/fauna), **Mama Nina** (fire/volcanoes), **Mama Wayra** (winds, purifier), **Apu** (mountain lords — every great peak has one, e.g., Ausangate, Salcantay), **Auquis** (regional guardians), **Paqtawañuy** (death; had a sanctuary in Cusco), **Puñuy** (dreams/sleep; also a Cusco sanctuary) (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_mythology).
- **Story hooks (general):** Regional gods let each game region have its own divine patron with distinct mechanics — Catequil's egg-twins for a duality puzzle, Ekeko's wish-dolls as a side-quest economy (place a miniature on the doll, receive it later — with ironic costs), the Apus as mountain "lords" who must each be petitioned to cross their territory (fast-travel gates with tribute mechanics), Qhoa as a sky-boss whose body parts are weather.

## 2. Inca Cosmology — the Three (or Six) Worlds & the Chakana

### The worlds
The familiar triad is **Hanan Pacha** (the world above — celestial: sun, moon, lightning, stars, rainbow), **Kay Pacha** (the world of here-and-now — humans, animals, plants), and **Uku Pacha** (the world within/below — the dead, the unborn, disease, the world of eternal darkness) (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_mythology).
- **Uku Pacha is not a hell.** It is the realm of fertility, the past, and the ancestors — "where the seeds dwell before they sprout into the sunlight of Kay Pacha" — connected to our world through *paqarinas* (sacred caves, springs, lagoons of emergence) (`index`, https://vectree.io/pdf/c/andean-cosmological-layers).
- **Scholarly caveat [scholarly consensus]:** the six-world Andean model (adding **Hawa Pacha**, the outside/imperceptible world; **Hakaq/Haqay Pacha**, the spirit world; and **Hurin Pacha**, the world below) was likely **simplified to three levels by Spanish colonizers to fit Christian symbolism** (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_mythology). The "three worlds" every tourist brochure cites is partly a colonial artifact — a fact the game can use honestly (see hook).
- The animal triad: **condor = Hanan Pacha** (sky/divine), **puma = Kay Pacha** (earthly strength), **serpent/amaru = Uku Pacha** (underworld wisdom) (`index`, https://inkatimetours.com/inca-chakana-cross-history-meaning-inca-symbols/; https://peruvisit.com/peruscope/chakana/).
- Core principles: **yanantin** — complementary duality (male/female, sun/moon; nothing exists *ch'ulla*, alone/disparate); **tawantin** — the fourfold union of dualities (`index`, http://qentiwasi.com/2015/07/29/the-tawantin-inside-and-out/).

### The chakana (stepped cross)
- A square stepped cross with four equal arms, **three steps per arm, twelve outer points, and a central circular hole** (read as Cusco, the navel, or the sun). "Chakana" means *bridge*. It is also the Quechua name for the **Southern Cross** constellation, believed to be the center of the universe. Modern readings map the three steps to the three pachas, the four arms to the cardinal directions/four suyus, the twelve points to the twelve months, and the hole to the axis mundi (`index`, https://inkatimetours.com/inca-chakana-cross-history-meaning-inca-symbols/; https://www.southworld.net/bolivia-chakana-the-andean-cosmic-bridge/).
- **Critical accuracy flag [modern interpretation]:** Wikipedia's Inca mythology article states plainly: **"The alleged meaning of the *chakana* symbol is not supported by scholarly literature"** (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_mythology). The three-worlds/three-steps, twelve-months, and moral-triad (ama sua/ama llulla/ama quella) attributions are **modern and revivalist**, not documented pre-conquest doctrine. The game can use the symbol freely — but should not present these meanings as excavated fact. (The triad *ama sua, ama llulla, ama quella* — do not steal, lie, or be lazy — is genuinely recorded in chronicler tradition; only its mapping onto the chakana is modern.)
- **Story hook:** This caveat is a gift. Make the chakana a **contested symbol**: a new-age guide character explains the three-worlds reading with total confidence, while Naira (the archaeologist) knows it's modern invention — and the *real* mechanism of an ancient chakana-carved door works on a principle neither expects (e.g., the Southern Cross alignment). The central hole as "navel/axis" makes every chakana a potential fast-travel or world-crossing gate: "chakana = bridge" is the literal level-transition device. A three-stepped descent (three levels, three trials) structures an underworld chapter honestly echoing Uku Pacha as ancestor-realm, not hell.

## 3. Foundation Myths — Manco Capac, Mama Ocllo, the Ayar Brothers, Pacaritambo

There are **three competing origin legends**, and the sources themselves explain why: commoners were forbidden to speak Viracocha's name, so the empire needed parallel stories (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_mythology).

**Legend A — the Ayar siblings (the "official" migration myth).** Eight siblings emerged from the chief window (*qhapaq t'uqu*) of the cave-hill **Paqariq Tampu (Pacaritambo)** — "house of dawning/origin" — about 25 km south of Cusco: brothers **Ayar Manco (Manco Capac), Ayar Auca, Ayar Cachi, Ayar Uchu** and sisters **Mama Ocllo, Mama Huaco, Mama Raua, Mama Ipacura**. The hill had three windows: the Maras tribe emerged from *Maras T'uqu*, the Tampus from *Sut'i T'uqu*, the royal siblings from the middle. Carrying a **golden staff** (*tapac-yauri*) given by their father, they were told to settle where it sank into the earth. On the journey Manco eliminated his brothers — **Ayar Cachi was tricked into re-entering a cave and sealed inside** (his cruelty angered the tribes they courted); **Ayar Uchu and Ayar Auca were turned to stone** (one became the huaca of Huanacauri hill, marking possession of the land). Manco married his elder sister **Mama Ocllo**; their son was Sinchi Roca. The Inca Pachacuti later venerated Pacaritambo itself, fitting **doors of gold** on the chief window and making it a *wak'a* for oracles and sacrifice (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_mythology; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paqariq_Tampu; `index`, https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/manco-capac-and-mama-ocllo).

**Legend B — children of the Sun (Garcilaso's version).** Manco Capac and Mama Ocllo as **son and daughter of Inti (and Mama Killa)**, sent up from the waters of Lake Titicaca / the Island of the Sun, emerging with the golden staff to found the Temple of the Sun where it sank. Manco was worshipped as a fire and sun god; on his death he said **"I must return to the sky, for my father has summoned me."** **Authenticity flag [disputed]:** no written tradition of this tale predates Garcilaso de la Vega's *Comentarios Reales* (1609); scholars question whether it is a genuine Inca legend or Garcilaso's literary construction — possibly a late myth crafted to fold the powerful Aymara of Titicaca into the empire (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_mythology; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manco_C%C3%A1pac).

**Legend C — son of Viracocha.** Manco as son of "Inca Viracocha" of Paqariq Tampu, uniting the ten ayllus encountered on the migration to conquer the Cusco valley; in some tellings the young Manco jealously betrayed and killed his brothers outright.

**The relics:** Manco's mummified body was adored as a *huaca*; the Spaniards hunted it but never found it — it was kept in a village outside Cusco and was **said to have turned into stone**, elaborately dressed and adorned, becoming one of the holiest objects in the Inca world (`index`, https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/manco-capac-and-mama-ocllo). Mama Ocllo taught women to weave cloth and build houses — the civilizing counterpart to Manco's conquest.

**Story hooks:**
- The golden staff that sinks only in the right place is a dowsing-rod quest item — Naira must carry a replica (or the original) and find where it "wants" to sink; Sol Negro has a forgery that sinks everywhere, a nice tell.
- The three windows = a triple-gated dungeon: each sibling-band's window holds a trial; the sealed cave of Ayar Cachi is a tomb level (the brother walled up alive, still "angry").
- Manco's never-found stone mummy is the ultimate MacGuffin alternative to Paititi: the holiest huaca the Spanish never destroyed, still dressed and waiting. Finding it could be the game's true ending.
- Legend B's disputed status is a meta-plot device: a 1609 manuscript (Garcilaso) that may be fabrication — Naira must decide whether to trust a colonial text or the archaeology.

## 4. Sacred Geography

**Cusco — the navel of the world.** Capital of Tawantinsuyu ("the four parts together"): **Chinchaysuyu** (NW), **Antisuyu** (NE), **Qollasuyu** (SE), **Kuntisuyu** (SW), split into Hanan (upper) and Hurin (lower) moieties. The city's plan was read as a **puma**, with Sacsayhuamán as its head and its zigzag walls as the teeth; it was also the convergence point of the ceque lines and the empire's roads (`index`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceque_system; https://latintrails.com/top-10-inca-ruins/).

**The ceque system.** 41–42 ritual lines (*seq'e*, "line") radiating from the Coricancha, strung with **328 *huacas*** (shrines) — springs, boulders, caves, fountains, canals, buildings. Each line was maintained by specific kin groups (*ayllus*); huacas were ranked **Collana / Payan / Cayao**. Functions layered: pilgrimage routes, irrigation-canal cleaning rites, social-territorial organization — and, per Tom Zuidema's calendrical reading, a ritual calendar (41 lines ≈ 41 weeks; 328 huacas ≈ 328 nights). Brian Bauer's 1990–95 field survey (*The Sacred Landscape of the Inca*, 1998) called it "the most complex indigenous ritual system in the pre-Columbian Americas" and found ~30% of huacas were water sources (`index`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceque_system; https://www.ubcpress.ca/the-sacred-landscape-of-the-inca).

**Coricancha (Quri Kancha, "golden enclosure").** The most important temple in the empire and "the very center of the Inca world." Walls sheathed in gold sheets; a great golden sun disk; a courtyard garden of gold and silver plants, animals, and human figures. Separate chapels: the Sun (llama sacrifice), the **Moon** (silver murals, Mama Quilla), **Venus and the Stars** (astronomical observation for the agricultural calendar), the **Rainbow** (link of sun and earth), **Thunder/Lightning/Rain** (Illapa) — plus fountains, purification channels, and an observatory whose windows tracked solstices and equinoxes. Pachacuti rebuilt and enriched it; the mummies of seven deceased Incas sat on a golden bench in gold masks. The Spanish stripped and melted everything, gave the shell to the Dominicans, and built the church/convent of Santo Domingo on top — whose walls later fell in earthquakes while the Inca ashlar masonry stood (`index`, http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coricancha; https://machupicchusacred.com/en/cusco/archaeological-centers/qorikancha/; http://enperublog.com/2006/11/04/qoricancha-templo-del-sol/).

**Sacsayhuamán.** The puma's head above Cusco: colossal zigzag limestone walls; both fortress and ceremonial temple (Illapa prominent); the Incas' final stand against the Spanish; since 1944, the stage of the Inti Raymi reenactment (`index`, https://latintrails.com/top-10-inca-ruins/).

**Ollantaytambo.** Fortress-town of the Sacred Valley; the Sun Temple's six great monoliths; site of Manco Inca's **January 1537 victory over the Spanish** — the largest Inca battlefield success of the conquest — before his retreat to the jungle (`index`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manco_Inca_Yupanqui via search).

**Machu Picchu.** Royal estate of Pachacuti (c. 1430); the *Intihuatana* ritual stone ("hitching post of the sun"); the Temple of the Sun with its solstice window; structures aligned to sun and constellations. Brought to world attention by Hiram Bingham in 1911 (who thought it might be Vilcabamba). One archaeoastronomical reading (Zuidema/Magli) treats it as a node in solstice pilgrimages along the Vilcanota river — the river as the Milky Way's terrestrial image (`index`, https://www.perurail.com/blog-en/inti-raymi-an-ancestral-celebration-in-the-heart-of-cusco/; search summary of Magli's pilgrimage paper).

**Lesser-known sites (fresh territory for level design):**
- **Pachacamac** (coast, near Lima) — pre-Inca Ichma oracle and pilgrimage center absorbed by the Incas; the two-faced wooden idol; statue destroyed by Hernando Pizarro in 1533.
- **Choquequirao** — "the sister of Machu Picchu," remote, far less excavated.
- **Sayhuite monolith** — a great carved stone that models an entire ritual/irrigation landscape in miniature.
- **Qenko** — monoliths and astronomically aligned structures; at the June solstice sunrise, light through a fissure sequentially illuminates carved gnomons — "the awakening of the puma."
- **Tipón** (hydraulic engineering), **Moray** (agricultural terrace laboratory), **Pisac**, **Llactapata**, **Winay Wayna** (Inca Trail), **Pusharo petroglyphs** and the **Paratoari "pyramids"** (shown by Thierry Jamin to be natural formations — with genuine Inca artifacts nearby), **Mameria** (documented Inca site complex deep in the high jungle) (`index`, https://colourfulperu.com/news/sacred-sites-of-the-incas/; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paititi).
- **Story hooks:** The ceque system is a ready-made open-world structure: 41 lines radiating from a hub, each with ranked shrines — a quest web with Collana/Payan/Cayao tiers as difficulty bands. Coricancha's stacked church-on-temple is the game's visual thesis (conquest literally built on sacred ground; earthquake reveals). Qenko's solstice light-puzzle is a real mechanic. Sayhuite's miniature landscape is a diegetic map-table: solve the model, find the real valley. And the Coricancha's chapel-per-deity layout lets each god "own" a wing of a dungeon.

## 5. The Paititi Legend — in Full

**The legend.** Paititi is a legendary Inca lost city or utopian rich land, allegedly hidden east of the Andes in the rainforests of southeast Peru, northern Bolivia, or northwest Brazil. In the Peruvian version it revolves around the culture-hero **Inkarri**: after founding Q'ero and Cusco, Inkarri retreated toward the Pantiacolla jungles to live out his days in his refuge city of Paititi. Other versions place it as an Inca refuge on the Bolivia–Brazil border (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paititi).

**Colonial paper trail.** Sixteenth-to-eighteenth-century sources reference Paititi repeatedly: Juan Álvarez Maldonado (1570), Gregorio Bolívar (1621), Juan Recio de León (1623–27), Juan de Ojeda (1676), Diego de Eguiluz (1696). In 2001, Italian archaeologist Mario Polia found missionary **Andrés López's report (c. 1600)** in the Jesuit archives in Rome: a great city rich in gold, silver, and jewels in the jungle, called Paititi by the natives, reported to the Pope — centering on a miracle performed at the king of Paititi's court by a crucifix carried by baptized Indians. **The report is third-hand and unreliable: López never reached Paititi; he only heard about it from natives** (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paititi).

**Modern research.**
- In 2001, Helsinki researchers Ari Siiriäinen and Martti Pärssinen hypothesized the legend grew from real Inca expeditions into the Amazon and a possible Inca military presence around the Beni and Madre de Dios rivers. Their 2001–2003 Finnish-Bolivian excavation at the fortified site of **Las Piedras** (near Riberalta, Bolivia) found fragments of imperial Inca ceramics — but "the presumed Inca origin of the site remains questionable" (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paititi).
- Historian-anthropologist Vera Tyuleneva has argued for the **non-Peruvian origin of the name "Paititi"** and done field expeditions in northern Bolivia (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paititi).
- Explorer Andrew Nicol's critical analysis of primary texts concluded a remote Inca jungle outpost "could theoretically exist" in the Peruvian Amazon, citing **Vilcabamba** and **Mameria** as the chief evidence; the region is the old **Antisuyu** quarter (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paititi).
- **Debunked in passing:** the 2007 "Manco Pata fortress" near Kimbiri (40,000 m² of walls) was ruled by Peru's National Institute of Culture to be **naturally formed sandstone** with no trace of human stonework (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paititi; `index`, https://www.peruviantimes.com/26/inc-dismisses-archeological-fortress-as-mythical-lost-city/201/).

**Expeditions (a century of obsession).** Percy Fawcett (1925, vanished); Hans Ertl (1954–55, Bolivia — terraces, walls, precious-metal artifacts, a gold mask); Carlos Neuenschwander Landa (1958–2003, Madre de Dios/Cusco); the French-American Nichols/Debru/Puel expedition (1971 — guides abandoned them up the Río Pantiacolla; all three killed); Gregory Deyermenjian (1984–2011; documented Mameria, the Pusharo petroglyphs, the Paratoari pyramids, and Inca ruins along the "Inca Road of Stone" at Último Punto); Lars Hafskjold (1997, disappeared); John Blashford-Snell (2001); Thierry Jamin (2001 — the Pantiacolla "pyramids" are natural, but Inca artifacts nearby; 2005 — Pusharo geoglyphs as a possible "map" to Paititi); Jacek Pałkiewicz (2002); Olly Steeds (2009–10); Josh Gates (2014, *Expedition Unknown*) (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paititi; `index`, https://www.splashtravels.com/destinations/search-for-lost-city-of-gold).

**What scholars actually believe [scholarly consensus].** There is no scholarly consensus that Paititi exists as a city. What *is* documented: genuine Inca presence on the Amazonian fringe (Mameria, Último Punto, Las Piedras ceramics), and — separately — the **real** last Inca refuge, **Vilcabamba** (§8). Paititi is most plausibly a conflation of the Inkarri myth, colonial El Dorado-style rumor, real Inca jungle outposts, and 20th-century explorer lore. **Label any in-game "we found Paititi" as fiction.**

**Related lost-city lore.** Vilcabamba (real Neo-Inca state, 1537–1572; its ruins at Espíritu Pampa) is the historical anchor — a game can contrast the *documented* lost city with the *mythical* one. El Dorado, Fawcett's "City of Z," and Akakor belong to the same rumor-family; **Akakor is [fringe]** with no scholarly support.

**Story hooks:**
- The López letter is a perfect Act I artifact: a 1600 Jesuit report, third-hand, describing a miracle at a golden king's court — Sol Negro treats it as a treasure map; Naira knows it's hearsay, but the *miracle* detail (a crucifix carried by baptized Indians) implies someone really went deep into the jungle and came back.
- Structure the hunt as competing theories: the Inkarri version (mythic), the Antisuyu version (military outpost), the Bolivia–Brazil version — three search zones, only one (or none) real. The game's "Paititi" can be Mameria-like: real Inca ruins that aren't the city, forcing the question of what the legend actually encodes.
- **Production warning:** *Shadow of the Tomb Raider* (2018) already made Paititi its key locale and name-checked López and Fawcett (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paititi). The team must differentiate — the Inkarri angle, the Vilcabamba contrast, and the "legend as conflation" thesis are all underused.
- **Archetypal overlap:** The hidden city is the classic *otherworld/axis mundi* archetype (Shambhala, the Grail castle, the City of the Caesars). Paititi's pull works because it fuses two archetypes: the **lost golden age** and the **underworld journey** — the jungle as the threshold to Uku Pacha.

## 6. Rituals and the Sacred Dead

**Inti Raymi — Festival of the Sun.** The empire's most important festival, established by Pachacuti (the first dated to 1412), held over **nine days at the June winter solstice** in Cusco's Haukaypata square; it marked the Andean new year and symbolized the mythical origin of the Inca people. Preparation: three days of fasting (a little raw white maize, *chúcan* herbs, water), **no fire lit anywhere in the city**, no sleeping with wives; the Virgins of the Sun baked maize bread. At dawn the Inca and his family, barefoot with arms open, greeted the rising sun; the Inca toasted with two gold cups of chicha; processions to Coricancha; provincial *curacas* delivered offerings; **mass llama sacrifice** before the sacred fire; meat and chicha distributed to the people; days of dance and feasting. The Spanish banned it (the last with an Inca emperor present: 1535). In **1944 Faustino Espinoza Navarro reconstructed it** from Garcilaso de la Vega's chronicles; it is now staged annually on June 24 at Qorikancha → Plaza de Armas → Sacsayhuamán, the second-largest festival in South America (`index`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inti_Raymi; https://machupicchusacred.com/en/blog/festivities-in-peru/inti-raymi/).

**Capacocha (*qhapaq hucha*) — the child sacrifices.** On rare, grave occasions — an emperor's death, a royal birth, a great victory, calendrical rites, or catastrophes (earthquake, eruption, epidemic) — the state sacrificed children chosen for **perfect beauty and health**; selection was an honor. Victims were gathered from across the empire, processed hundreds of miles to and from Cusco, then led up sacred peaks, **drugged with coca and maize beer** (hair analysis shows increasing doses over the final year of life), and placed in stone niches to freeze to death. Theology: the children did not die but **joined the ancestors as messengers**, watching over their villages "like angels," appeasing mountain deities and securing the new emperor's reign (`index`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Children_of_Llullaillaco; http://www.scientificamerican.com/article/incan-child-sacrificed-to-the-gods-reveals-history-of-american-expansion/; https://gizmodo.com/inca-children-were-drunk-and-stoned-before-ritual-sacri-978304562).
- The **Children of Llullaillaco** (found 16 March 1999 by Johan Reinhard and Constanza Ceruti at 6,739 m on the Argentina–Chile border): a 15-year-old girl (*La Doncella*, likely an *acllakuna*/chosen woman), a 7-year-old boy, and a 6-year-old girl, sacrificed c. 1462–1507 — among the best-preserved mummies ever found, displayed at Salta's Museum of High Altitude Archaeology since 2007. They were buried with 100+ sumptuary offerings: textiles, gold and silver figurines (gold = *qori*, silver = *collqe*, the manifested divinity of sun and moon), pottery, food, feather headdresses.
- Newer findings complicate the "peaceful sleep" narrative: CT scans of the **El Plomo boy** (Chile, sacrificed c. 1460 after walking 2,000+ km from south-central Peru) show a skillfully executed **blunt-force blow to the skull**; a 2025 study of four Peruvian-volcano children found fatal head blows, with bodies later revered and even relocated (`index`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Children_of_Llullaillaco; https://Inshorts.com/en/news/incas-sacrificed-kids-with-fatal-head-blows--then-revered-their-mummified-remains-1771941821074; Yahoo News summary of the El Plomo study).

**The mallqui — ancestor bundles and the living dead.** The Quechua *mallqui/mallki* ("seedling") was the mummy bundle of an ancestor. Royal mummification did not preserve the past — it **manufactured a living ancestor** who retained *camay* (vital force): the dead Sapa Inca, seated in fetal position and wrapped in fine *cumbi* cloth, **continued to own property, receive daily food and chicha from his *panaca* (descent group), attend festivals, and even be carried into battle to stiffen morale** — "with the same powers as the bodies of their owners when they were still alive" (chronicler Bernabé Cobo). Rulers also had *wawki* ("brother") simulacra as physical extensions. The dead were housed in *machays* (caves/rock niches) — portals between Uku Pacha and Kay Pacha — kept accessible for rites. After the conquest, the Spanish **extirpation of idolatries** (c. 150 years of campaigns) confiscated the royal mummies (1559; carried weeping through Cusco to a Lima hospital), invaded crypt-caves, and burned the most "threatening" mummies — because the ancestor cult was, in Cobo's words, the single greatest obstacle to conversion. Andean communities secretly dug their dead out of church cemeteries and returned them to the ancestral caves (`index`, https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/12/5/287; http://www.smithsonianmag.com/travel/fascinating-afterlife-perus-mummies-180956319/).
- Afterlife beliefs (chronicler accounts): the spirit (*camaquen*) traveled a long road needing **a black dog that could see in the dark**; one must not die by burning, lest the vital force scatter; those who kept the moral code (*ama sua, ama llulla, ama quella* — do not steal, lie, be lazy) would "live in the Sun's warmth," others in the cold earth.

**Story hooks:**
- The mallqui system makes ancestors *political actors*: a mummy that owns land is a legal obstacle — imagine Sol Negro's lawyers vs. a 500-year-old property owner. A *wawki* double lets you do a body-swap or decoy plot.
- Capacocha sites are the game's most ethically charged locations: pristine, high-altitude, heartbreaking. Treat them with gravity — Naira as Peruvian archaeologist can articulate why these children were honored, not merely victimized; Sol Negro looting a capacocha niche is an unambiguous villain beat. The buried offering assemblages (gendered figurines, qori/collqe) are authentic loot-table material.
- The extirpation campaigns give a historical layer for "hidden mummy" side quests: a bundle concealed from the priests, still tended in secret.
- The black-dog psychopomp pairs with Illapa's black dogs (§1): the same animal that summons rain guides the dead. A dog companion with underworld sight is mythologically licensed.
- **Archetypal overlap:** The honored, consulted dead rhyme with the universal **ancestor cult** and the **underworld journey** — but the Inca version is distinctive: the dead are not distant shades but *landlords*. The Book of Symbols' framing of death/rebirth as transformation (phoenix, ouroboros) maps onto *camay*: death as a change of state, not an end.

## 7. Mythic Beings and Creatures

**The Amaru.** In Andean mythology the *amaru* (Aymara *katari*) is a mythical serpent or dragon — in Inca telling, a **huge, sometimes double-headed serpent dwelling underground and at the bottom of lakes and rivers**. It embodies wisdom, water, earth, and the underworld; infinity, knowledge, and cosmic renewal; it **traverses worlds**, crossing between the earthly and spiritual realms. It is ambivalent: associated both with **earthquakes** and with the **irrigating rivers** the highland peoples depended on. Serpent carvings at Qorikancha link it to underground passages and ancestral knowledge (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_mythology; `index`, https://inkatimetours.com/sacred-animals-in-inca-mythology/).
- **Archetypal overlap:** This is the textbook *Book of Symbols* serpent — "transformation, healing, primal energy; both danger and wisdom, depending on context" — and the double head evokes the ouroboros (eternity, cyclic renewal). The amaru's world-crossing also parallels the rainbow serpent and the Mesoamerican Quetzalcoatl (feathered serpent joining sky and earth). Note the name's afterlife: **Túpac Amaru** = *thupaq* (royal/shining) + *amaru* (serpent) — "royal serpent."
- **Story hook:** A double-headed serpent whose two heads disagree (destroyer vs. irrigator) is a boss with a moral mechanic: feed the benevolent head, starve the wrathful one. As a world-crosser, the amaru is the diegetic justification for underworld fast-travel — follow the serpent's tunnels between Kay and Uku Pacha.

**Yakumama (Yacumama).** "Mother of water / mother of all rivers" — an Amazonian **anaconda goddess** absorbed into Quechua mythology: guardian of the waterways. In the Andes her image fragments across the water goddess, the siren, the devouring lagoon, the serpentine river, Illapa, the amaru, and a **constellation** — a genuine documented case of lowland–highland mythic fusion (Kerstin Störl's semantic-cultural analysis, 2016) (`index`, http://biblioteca-repositorio.clacso.edu.ar:8080/xmlui/handle/CLACSO/151269).
- **Story hook:** Perfect for the jungle chapters: the river itself is a goddess. Rapids as her moods; a "mother of rivers" shrine at a headwater.

**Others:** **Supay** (death god, later demonized by missionaries); **Qhoa** (giant winged sky-feline: lightning from its eyes, thunder its roar, rain its urine); **Hurkaway**, guardian snake of Uku Pacha, identified with **Urcaguary**, deity of jewels and metals — a treasure-guardian by definition; the condor/puma/serpent trilogy; the Milky Way as a celestial river stocked with dark-cloud animal constellations (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_mythology).
- **Story hook:** Urcaguary the jewel-god's serpent is the in-world reason treasure is *cursed*: Sol Negro stealing gold isn't just theft, it's taking the serpent-god's hoard. The dark-cloud constellations give you a "read the dark, not the stars" navigation puzzle — Inca astronomy worked on the black shapes between stars, which most players (and most games) have never used.

## 8. The Conquest as Mythic Backdrop

**Cajamarca & the ransom room (1532–33).** On 16 November 1532 Francisco Pizarro's ~168 Spaniards ambushed Atahualpa's court at Cajamarca, massacring thousands of Inca nobility in about two hours without losing a man. Atahualpa, grasping the Spaniards' hunger for gold, offered to fill a room **22 × 17 feet (6.7 × 5.17 m) to a height of ~8 feet (2.45 m) once with gold and twice with silver**. A notary formalized it; caravans of gold and silver artworks poured in from across the empire (the Spaniards smashed pieces so the room would fill *slower*). The ransom totaled some **13,000 pounds of gold and twice that in silver — all melted down**, annihilating an irreplaceable artistic heritage. Pizarro broke the bargain: Atahualpa was tried on trumped-up charges and executed — **26 July 1533** (ThoughtCo) or **29 August 1533** (other sources) — strangled/garroted after a sham trial, amid rumors of a rescue army under Rumiñahui (`index`, https://www.thoughtco.com/the-ransom-of-atahualpa-2136547; https://www.thoughtco.com/capture-of-inca-atahualpa-2136546; http://www.discover-peru.org/the-fall-of-the-inca-empire/).
- **Rumiñahui's gold** (hidden in Ecuador's Llanganates mountains on news of the execution) is **[folklore]**-grade treasure lore — usable, but label it legend.

**Manco Inca's rebellion (1536–37).** Crowned as a Spanish puppet, Manco Inca was brutalized by the Pizarro brothers, escaped, raised **100,000–200,000 warriors**, and **besieged Cusco for ten months** (early 1536) while his captain Quiso Yupanqui besieged Lima — killing ~500 Spaniards and destroying four relief columns. Forced back, he won the **Battle of Ollantaytambo (January 1537)**, then withdrew to Vitcos and into the cloud forests of **Vilcabamba**, founding the **Neo-Inca State (1537/40–1572)**. The Spanish crowned his half-brother Paullu Inca as puppet; Manco's sister-wife **Cura Ocllo was murdered in 1539**; Manco himself was killed in **1544** (`index`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manco_Inca_Yupanqui; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Inca_State).

**The fall of Vilcabamba (1572).** Succession ran Sayri Túpac (accepted Spanish authority 1558, died 1561, possibly poisoned) → Titu Cusi (d. 1571) → **Túpac Amaru**. Viceroy Toledo's war of annihilation ended with Captain Martín García de Loyola dragging Túpac Amaru in chains from the jungle to Cusco (21 September 1572); after a sham trial and hasty baptism he was **beheaded in Cusco's main square on 24 September 1572**, guarded by 400 Cañari warriors — "great enemies of the Incas" — ending the political Inca state (`index`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Túpac_Amaru; http://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/69003/excerpt/9780521869003_excerpt.pdf; http://public-library.uk/ebooks/13/34.pdf). Bingham (1911) mistook Machu Picchu for Vilcabamba; the true site, **Espíritu Pampa**, was later identified — the *documented* lost city against which Paititi must be measured.

**Story hooks:**
- The ransom room's exact dimensions (a real, documented room) make a superb "reconstruction" puzzle: Naira finds the room's foundations at Cajamarca; the fill-line on the wall is a measuring device for a cache hidden *by volume*. The melted art is Sol Negro's ideological mirror — the first great antiquities crime, committed by the Spanish, which the ring knowingly echoes.
- Manco's ten-month siege + the Ollantaytambo victory give an all-Indigenous war chapter (flashback or discovered battlefield). Cura Ocllo's murder and the 400 Cañari guards at the execution supply named human stakes.
- 1532–1572 is a 40-year resistance, not a single defeat — frame the conquest as a long rearguard action, with Vilcabamba as a functioning jungle state (palaces, farms, Spanish renegades at court), not a ruin. Its fall is the game's historical "original sin" layer beneath the modern plot.
- The execution-date discrepancy (July 26 vs. August 29, 1533) is a nice authenticity touch: let two in-game sources disagree, and let Naira notice.

## 9. Quipu, Solar Alignments, and Stonework as Mystery Devices

**Quipu (*khipu*, "knot").** Knotted-string recording devices of cotton/wool: pendant cords on a main cord, read left-to-right; **color, knot type and position, torsion, and twist direction** all encoded information in a **base-10** system (absence of a knot = zero). Kept and read by specialist administrators, the *khipukamayoq*. They recorded census, taxes, tribute, storehouse inventories, calendric and astronomical cycles — and, chroniclers claimed, laws, histories, and royal exploits. The Spanish burned the archives; **no quipu's specific meaning has been fully deciphered** (`index`, https://smarthistory.org/inka-khipu/; https://www.sciencenews.org/article/khipus-knotted-cords-incas-taxes).
- Breakthroughs: at **Inkawasi** (2013), 29 khipus buried under peanuts and chili peppers in a storehouse provided the first archaeological evidence of an Inca **tax system** just before the conquest. Sabine Hyland's study of ~100 quipus preserved by the community of **Santa Leonor de Jucul** found a **68-meter quipu** (largest ever, c. 1480 by radiocarbon of braided-in hair) recording **climate events** — droughts, seasonal shifts, and the religious responses (offerings at rain shrines). Makers **signed their work by braiding their own hair into the cords**, and diet analysis showed they were **local villagers, not imperial scribes** — quipu literacy was communal, not elite (`index`, https://newsmaven.io/the-mysteries-of-a-writing-system-of-the-inca-empire-finally-begin-to-be-pierced/).
- Gary Urton's theory that quipus encode **binary/phonological data** is **[unverified]** but tantalizing (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_mythology).
- **Story hook:** The quipu is the game's cipher system by divine right: color + knot + twist = combination locks; a climate quipu is a drought-prophecy ("when the cords say dry, the shrine must be fed"); hair-signatures let Naira identify *who* recorded a message — a 500-year-old "signature." An undeciphered quipu can honestly remain partially unreadable — the game never has to fake a full translation.

**Solar & celestial alignments.** Coricancha's windows were aligned to **solstices and equinoxes**; stone pillars around Cusco marked the sun's position; the Incas ran **two calendars** — solar and lunar/stellar — with specialist observers. At **Qenko**, the June-solstice sunrise shines through a fissure to sequentially light carved gnomons — "the awakening of the puma." Machu Picchu's **Intihuatana** and Temple of the Sun window encode solstice sightlines. Zuidema's calendrical reading of the ceques (41 lines, 328 huacas) ties ritual geography to timekeeping (`index`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_mythology [Coricancha observatory]; https://artsandculture.google.com/story/inca-religion-and-archaeoastronomy-universidad-nacional-de-san-antonio-abad-del-cusco-unsaac/jwWRECr11WMRIQ?hl=en; search summary of Qenko via Wikipedia Inca mythology).
- **Story hook:** Solstice-locked doors (the game can honestly use the June-21/December-21 dates); pillar-shadow puzzles; a two-calendar mechanic where solar and lunar solutions must be reconciled.

**Stonework.** **Ashlar masonry** — uniform cut cuboids laid without mortar; at Coricancha only flawless stones were accepted; **trapezoidal** doors/windows; walls leaning slightly inward for earthquake resistance. Fine masonry was **imperial propaganda**: its labor cost demonstrated the state's power to mobilize workers, and the style was replicated across the Andes as a signature of control. Sacsayhuamán's megalithic zigzag walls; the **Sayhuite monolith**, a carved miniature ritual landscape (`index`, http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coricancha; https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wUin4lrbR9U [Coricancha masonry summary]).
- **Story hook:** Masonry *quality* as a dating/authenticity tool: Naira distinguishes imperial ashlar from provincial or modern work — Sol Negro's forgeries fail the stone test. Trapezoids as a visual language for "Inca-made" vs. "older/pre-Inca" (e.g., Tiwanaku's Gate of the Sun, which already bore sun-god imagery). Sayhuite's model-landscape is a literal 3D map puzzle.

## 10. Regional Variations and Obscure Myths

The empire was a mosaic; the richest strangeness lives outside Cusco's state cult.

- **The Huarochirí Manuscript** (17th c., Quechua): the fullest surviving record of *provincial* Andean religion — "as close as we are likely to come to a purely Andean expression of the supernatural" (Salomon & Urioste). Its trickster cycle — **Cuniraya Huiracocha, Huatiacuri, Paryaqaqa**, and the goddess **Chaupiñamca** (feminine counterpart of Paryaqaqa, "a rigid stone with five wings") — is darker and earthier than the Inca state myths (`index`, https://www.ubcpress.ca/the-huarochiri-manuscript).
- **Inkarri** (see §5): the executed Inca-king (*Inca-rey*) whose dismembered body — head under Lima's Presidential Palace, arms under Cusco's Waqaypata ("Square of Tears"), legs in Ayacucho — **grows underground until he rises** to restore harmony between Pachamama and her children. Paititi is said to be his foundation (`index`, http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inkarri).
- **The Cavillace fruit** (§1): a virgin eats a fruit, a baby crawls to its disguised father, mother and child become coastal rocks — a complete three-act tragedy in one myth.
- **Kon vs. Pachakamaq** (§1): the boneless flying-feline creator deposed by the earthquake god — a Titanomachy for the Andes.
- **Huallallo vs. Paryaqaqa** (§1): the child-eating fire god and the storm god turned into *adjacent snowy mountains* — enemies frozen side by side forever, still visible on the horizon.
- **Catequil's egg-birth** and his evil twin Piguerao (§1); **Ekeko's wish-dolls**; the **guano god Huamancantac** (coastal fertilizer economics made divine); Cusco's own shrines to **death (Paqtawañuy)** and **dreams (Puñuy)**; **Mama Kuka's** violent origin; **Coquena**, the camelid-god "husband of Pachamama" among the Diaguita-Calchaquí (`verified live`, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_mythology).
- **Story hooks:** Each region gets its own mythic texture so the Andes never feel monocultural: the north (Catequil's thunder-twins), the coast (Kon's sandstorms, Pachacamac's backwards oracle), Huarochirí (trickster gods), the south (Inkarri's growing body — a literal "the king is assembling himself" endgame threat or hope). The Inkarri body-parts map is a ready-made multi-region quest: head/arms/legs, three cities, one resurrection.

## 11. Archetypal Overlaps — Inca Symbols in Comparative Perspective

*Per the follow-up brief: brief public-source summaries of standard archetype references (Taschen's The Book of Symbols, ARAS/Jungian framing), mapped against the Inca material above. No book text reproduced.*

**Reference in brief.** *The Book of Symbols: Reflections on Archetypal Images* (eds. Ronnberg & Martin, Archive for Research in Archetypal Symbolism, TASCHEN, 2010; ~350 essays, 800+ images) treats symbols across all cultures and eras through a Jungian lens — each entry examining a symbol's psychic background, etymological roots, play of opposites, and shadow aspects. Public summaries of its entries gloss: **serpent** = transformation, healing, primal energy — "both danger and wisdom, depending on context"; fertility/rebirth in some cultures, evil in others; the **ouroboros** = eternity and cyclic renewal. **Sun** = vitality, consciousness, enlightenment. **Moon** = mystery, femininity, cycles. **Water** = purification, subconscious depths, renewal. **Tree** = life and connection between worlds. **Cross** = sacrifice, intersection, spiritual connection. **Mandala** = wholeness, unity, cosmos (`index`, https://www.amazon.com/dp/3836514486/ref=as_li_ss_tl; https://www.abebooks.com/9783836514484/Book-Symbols-Reflections-Archetypal-Images-978383651X/plp).

**Genuine rhymes (safe to use as thematic bridges):**
1. **Amaru ↔ the archetypal serpent.** The amaru's documented ambivalence — earthquake-bringer *and* irrigation-giver, wisdom *and* underworld terror, world-crosser — is almost a literal illustration of the Book of Symbols' "danger and wisdom depending on context." Its double head reaching both ways evokes the ouroboros. The design team can lean on serpent-as-transformation without inventing anything.
2. **Inti ↔ the solar archetype.** Divine kingship through solar descent (Inca–Inti) structurally matches Pharaoh–Ra and the Japanese imperial line; the solar values of consciousness/order/enlightenment match Inti's civilizing role (Manco Capac teaching agriculture, law, and ritual).
3. **Pachamama ↔ the Great Mother.** Nourishing yet punitive earth-mother is one of the most stable cross-cultural archetypes (Gaia, Tonantzin, Bhudevi); Pachamama's spiral even echoes the archetypal spiral of growth and return.
4. **Uku Pacha ↔ the underworld journey.** The *katabasis* (descent and return) is universal — but the Inca version has a distinctive flavor worth preserving: the underworld is **fertility and ancestry, not punishment**, and it is entered through *paqarinas* (caves/springs), the same portals by which ancestors *emerged*. Naira's descent should feel like going to the source, not into hell.
5. **Paititi ↔ the hidden city / lost golden age.** Shambhala, the Grail castle, El Dorado: the archetype fuses a *place* (axis mundi) with a *time* (the age before the fall). Paititi's specific charge comes from its double nature — Inkarri's refuge *and* the ransom's melted gold — i.e., paradise fused with plunder.
6. **Chakana ↔ cross/mandala.** Formally it does the work of both archetypes: the cross as intersection of worlds ("chakana = bridge") and the mandala as cosmos-in-miniature (four arms, center point). **But** per the scholarly caveat in §2, the team should present its *form* as authentic and its *readings* as contested — characters can disagree about it, which is dramatically richer than a fixed meaning.
7. **The mallqui ↔ the consulted dead.** Ancestor veneration is universal; the Inca specificity — the dead as *property-owning political persons* — has no clean archetypal parallel, which makes it the freshest material in the dossier. Lean into what's *unlike* the archetype.

**Where the archetype breaks (use with care):** Illapa's dog-starving rain rite, capacocha, and the wawki doubles have no comfortable universal gloss — they are culturally specific and should be staged as such, not sanded down to "storm god" and "sacrifice" generics. The southern-hemisphere solstice inversion (June = winter, the sun at its weakest when Inti Raymi *begins the year*) is a productive estrangement device for northern-hemisphere players.

## Could Not Verify / Open Questions
1. **Chakana's pre-conquest meaning** — no scholarly support for the popular three-worlds/three-steps reading; treat as modern attribution.
2. **Atahualpa's execution date** — 26 July 1533 vs. 29 August 1533 across sources.
3. **Paititi's existence** — no scholarly consensus; documented facts end at real jungle outposts (Mameria, Último Punto, Las Piedras ceramics) and the real refuge (Vilcabamba).
4. **Urton's binary quipu theory** — intriguing, unverified.
5. **Ransom-room totals** — ~13,000 lbs gold / double silver is the chronicler-derived figure; exact amounts vary by source.
6. **Rumiñahui's hidden gold (Llanganates)** — treasure folklore, not archaeology.
7. **Pachacamac–Señor de los Milagros continuity** — popular claim, not verified in scholarly sources consulted.
8. **The 4,000 retainers sacrificed at Huayna Capac's death (1527)** — a single chronicler-derived claim found in one source; included only as an unverified report.
9. **Shadow of the Tomb Raider's Paititi treatment** — verified that it exists and name-checks López/Fawcett, but its specific plot details were not researched (out of scope); the design team should review it directly for differentiation.
